The Economic Circle

A plain-language map of where resources and NOVA come from, where they go, and why they flow. If the rest of the spec melted your brain, start here.

Two substances, one rule

The whole economy is built from just two things, and one rule connects them.

The one rule that ties them together: you can't mine everything you need, so you must trade or raid for the rest — and every trade or raid burns NOVA. Elements are what you want; NOVA is what it costs to move them.

Follow one player through a loop

1. Mine. Your planet produces ERBIUM and LANTHANUM well, but almost no THORIUM (soft scarcity). Ships and a research lab you want both need THORIUM.

2. Get NOVA. You have surplus ERBIUM. You sell some to the Company; it burns the ERBIUM and mints you NOVA. (Or, if you're in a hurry, you buy NOVA with SUI.)

3. Acquire what you lack. You spend NOVA as fuel to run a P2P trade — your ERBIUM for a neighbour's THORIUM — or, if nobody will deal, you burn NOVA to launch a compliance raid and take THORIUM at a defender-favourable rate. Either way, NOVA is the cost of moving goods between planets.

4. Build. You now have THORIUM on-planet. You spend it (plus your other elements) to build the ship and the lab. Those elements are consumed.

5. Repeat — bigger. Better mines and ships mean more surplus, more NOVA, more reach. Cash some NOVA out to SUI if you want to realize the gains, or reinvest it as fuel to expand.

That is the whole game loop. Everything else — storage, colonization, the tech tree — is a variation on "produce, sell for NOVA, spend NOVA to get what you lack, build."

What's settled vs. what we still choose

The structure is locked — the map above is decided. The remaining choices were about keeping NOVA valuable, funding the game, and staying fair against bots, and this session resolved all three.

Locked decided

  • Elements are mined in-game, global type, withdraw-only; the Company is sell-only (buys elements for NOVA, never sells them back).
  • NOVA is minted by selling elements, priced by a self-correcting per-element controller — an element sold more becomes cheaper, one sold less becomes dearer — burned as fuel, and traded for SUI on DeepBook.
  • You get elements you can't mine by trade or raid — both burn NOVA. Raids reach pending output, so nothing can be permanently cornered.
  • Planets differ (first planet mines all three; colonies can lack one). Storage halts at cap. Colonize by chosen coordinates.
  • NOVA issuance is demand-gated: the mint ceiling tracks exogenous SUI flowing into the NOVA/SUI market (and pool depth), not element production — so emission tightens automatically when demand weakens. (Tying the cap to production is self-referential and wash-farmable — a flaw the revenue check caught; see below.)

Fairness vs. farmingDecided 2026-09-20: no bond. Creating a commander is free, and the first universe is free. Registering the same commander in each further universe costs NOVA (a structural sink and the pay-to-accelerate path; a sybil does not pay it, it just mints another free commander — so this is a progression tax, not the anti-farm gate). The anti-farm gate is dilution: each planet's share of the demand-gated NOVA emission is weighted by planet age and cumulative production — the same basis the spec already uses for raid immunity — so a fresh farm planet mints a sliver for weeks and farms compete with each other for one bounded budget. Starting shape in the spec: two linear ramps multiplied, w = min(1, age/T_AGE) × min(1, cumulative_production/P_REF), with T_AGE ≈ the newbie-immunity window. Alt-boosting and bullying are blocked by a level-band interaction rule: commanders far apart in progression cannot trade with or raid each other. Why not a bond: a percentage cut can never make a zero-marginal-cost farm unprofitable, and the drip already makes total supply sybil-invariant — sybil harm is distributional, which dilution answers directly. Trade-price floors were considered and rejected (they break legitimate forward trades). Community-defined trade contracts (EVE-style) noted for Phase 3+.

How the game earns

You spotted the real flaw: if the Company buys elements and destroys them, where's the revenue? The answer is that the game never earns from elements — it earns from NOVA and fees. Elements are just supply that needs a sink; the money is in the monopoly on NOVA.

  • Seigniorage — the monopoly edge. The Company mints a small cut of all NOVA it issues into its own treasury. That treasury NOVA is worth real SUI to exactly the degree players pay SUI to acquire NOVA and accelerate — so as more people play with more agents, its value grows. This is your "excess NOVA from the monopolized market": the Company's fund.
  • Fees. The NOVA fee for registering a commander in a second and later universe, the NOVA→SUI cut above, and private-universe creation — influencers spin up universes for their communities and host events. A clear, scalable revenue line.
  • The element↔NOVA exchange stays clean — automated and unrigged. The game never profits by manipulating what your elements are worth; profit is seigniorage + fees only.

So must the Company burn the elements it buys? No — and revenue is identical either way, because it never came from elements. Two options: burn them (simplest, keeps elements scarce), or recycle them into the galaxy's shared reserves so they offset future minting (nothing "wasted", self-balancing supply). Decided (2026-09-20): recycle to a global pool — it matches the instinct that destroying resources feels wrong, and it doesn't reopen the buy-side bugs because the elements re-enter the shared drip, never a chosen planet.

Deliberately unfinished: elements & the tech tree

Three elements is the seed, not the ceiling. The element list should grow, and different tech should demand different combinations — bigger ships needing one mix, faster ships another, cheaper or quicker construction another — so specialisation and trade deepen as the tree expands. More elaborate build mechanics that stay in the trade-not-battle spirit are wanted here. Design against expansion from the start.

Good news on scope. None of these decisions change the map — they set the pressure and the business model, not the shape of the loop. And none block the first build step: fixing the existing bugs and standing up tests (Phase 0) is independent of all of it.
